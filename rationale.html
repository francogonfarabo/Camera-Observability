Camera health: two versions and why they differ

Design notes for a live board that tells a center admin, a regional manager, or an exec which cameras are down right now and how long they have been down — built at two different altitudes, so the altitude itself can be the thing you decide on.

Two versions

The same feature, placed at two different heights in the product. Everything else — visual language, status encoding, refresh behavior, accessibility — is deliberately identical, so the comparison is about placement and nothing else.

A · Nested — a tab inside the camera section

Camera Health becomes the first sub-tab of the existing Cameras area, beside Live, History, Saved Clips, and Admin Display. It still shows every center you can reach, but it lives inside the center-scoped part of the app.

Cheapest to ship and nothing about the shell changes. The cost is a conceptual seam: a tab sitting inside "the center you selected" that nonetheless reports on all centers. The header still names one center while the page talks about fourteen.
B · Fleet — a scope above center selection

Camera Health becomes the one thing that exists at account altitude, reached by a toggle inside the header's center-search field. Switching it on puts the app in focus mode: no center is selected, the center-scoped left nav and sub-nav are hidden, and the field reads "All my centers". Drilling into a center drops you back to normal center scope.

Resolves the seam honestly — the app stops claiming a center is selected when none is. The cost is real: a new scope in an app that has only ever had one, plus the shell work to hide and restore navigation.

My read: B is the right long-term model and A is the right first ship. The nested version gets the triage value into users' hands without touching global navigation; the fleet version is what it should become once the account scope is worth building. If the fleet view is likely within two quarters, build B now — retrofitting a scope later means re-teaching the navigation twice.

The job of the page

This is a monitoring surface, not a document: it gets scanned, not read. So every design decision below serves one of the four questions the brief asked it to answer, and each question is answered by a specific element rather than by "the dashboard" in general.

The questionWhat answers it
Which centers have the most cameras offline? Default sort + the count column. Centers rank by offline count, then degraded count, then outage length. Counts sit in their own column with tabular figures so the digits stack vertically and the worst center reads without parsing a sentence.
Which cameras have been offline the longest? The "Longest outage" tile and the per-row "Longest" line. Clicking the tile re-sorts the whole board by outage age, which is a different ranking from "most offline" and often the more urgent one.
Is this one camera actually down? The detail drawer — status, how long, when last checked, and a direct link to the live feed on my.wmg. The page reports a heartbeat; only the feed confirms a picture.
Show me only the centers I care about. Scope segment + filter bar. Scope switches between the center you're viewing and every center your role can reach; filters narrow by brand, state, parent-facing, and status.

Layout

One row per center_id, as specified. The row is three columns rather than the mockup's two:

Identity — name, state, store number, camera count

Plus a small eye glyph when the center is parent-facing, and an icon link out to my.wmg. Kept to two lines so every row has the same height.

Status counts — a fixed column of aligned numbers

"4 offline / 1 degraded / Longest 4h 05m", each on its own line with the number right-aligned in a 2-character box.

Why its own column: when the counts share the identity block they wrap unpredictably and the digits never line up. Given a column, ten rows of counts become a single scannable ladder — you find the worst center by looking down one narrow strip, not by reading ten sentences.
Camera grid — one block per camera, wrapping

20px blocks, top-aligned, in physical camera order so a room's block sits in the same place every visit. A compact density drops them to 14px when someone needs twenty centers on one screen.

A 3px left edge on each card carries the row's worst status, giving the list a scannable vertical margin before any text is read. Ten rows fit in about 810px, so a typical page needs one screen and a nudge.

The fleet version in detail

Version B is not a re-skin — it changes what the app claims about your position in it. Six decisions carry that difference.

The altitude is stated by exactly one control

In focus mode the field reads "All my centers" and its chip is filled blue. In center scope the chip goes quiet and the field carries the center's name. One control, two states, so there is never a moment where the header and the page disagree about where you are.

The center-scoped nav is hidden, not disabled

Dashboard, Requests, Users, Reports, and Settings all mean "for this center". At account altitude there is no this-center, so showing them would offer actions that cannot resolve.

Hiding rather than greying is the honest choice: a greyed nav reads as broken permissions, an absent nav reads as a different altitude. The sub-nav disappears for the same reason — at this height there is only one thing to look at, so tabs would be furniture.
The way in is a toggle inside the center-search field

The header's center field carries a "Camera health" affordance on its right edge. Off, it is quiet grey text. On, it becomes a filled #58A3CA chip with a dismiss ✕, the field's placeholder changes from "Search for center" to "All my centers", and the app enters focus mode.

Putting the toggle inside the control that already answers "which center am I looking at?" means one component owns scope. You do not learn a new place to look — you learn that the same control has a second position. The ✕ matters as much as the chip: an entered mode needs a visible exit, or it reads as being stuck.
A second, quieter way in from inside a center

A low-contrast "View across all centers" link sits at the far end of the per-center Cameras tab bar, for someone already in a center who wants to zoom out.

Deliberately text, not a button — it must not compete with Live / History / Saved Clips. Two entry points because there are two journeys: arriving cold with no center chosen, and realising mid-task that the question is bigger than the center you are in.
Squares, not tiles — and no streams at all

The board uses the same small status squares as version A: one per camera, colour plus texture, arranged in a wrapping grid per center. Clicking one opens a centered modal with that camera's status, duration, last-frame-received time, recorder, and a link out to my.wmg.

Squares beat thumbnails here twice over. They let a 20-camera center read as one glance instead of a scroll, and they make the streaming problem disappear by construction — a user with 14 centers would otherwise trigger 232 concurrent streams just by loading the page. Playback stays an explicit act on one camera, reached through the modal.
The modal is the detail surface, not a side panel

Version A uses a right-hand drawer; version B uses a centered modal with ← / → to walk the center's cameras and Esc to close, returning focus to the square you came from.

The drawer's advantage in version A was keeping the grid visible while you inspect. At account altitude the grid you would keep visible belongs to one center among fourteen, so the context worth preserving is weaker — and a modal reads as a harder stop, which suits a page you are triaging from rather than browsing.
There is a screen before any of this

The prototype opens on the normal dashboard with no center selected, which is why its camera panel reads "Error! Unable to load requested cameras." — there is genuinely nothing to fetch yet.

That empty state is the argument for the feature. The app's cold start is a screen that cannot answer anything until you pick one center out of fourteen; the toggle beside the search field is the shortest path from there to "what is broken anywhere?". Selecting a center from the dropdown fills the same panel with that center's cameras, so the contrast is visible in two clicks.
Healthy centers collapse to one row

Centers with a problem are listed individually with their full square grid shown. Everything healthy folds into a single expandable "7 centers healthy · 104 cameras reporting" row.

This is what makes the page work at scale. Equal-weight rows for 14 centers is a wall to be searched; problems-first with the good news compressed is a queue to be worked. The "All centers" toggle still gives the full list when someone wants to browse rather than triage. Problem centers show every camera, not only the broken ones — with squares that context is free, and four red among twenty green tells a different story than four red alone.
Correlated failures get named

When the offline cameras at a center share a recorder, the card says so: "All 4 offline cameras here run through recorder NVR-2 — likely one site network fault, not 4 separate camera failures." When only some of them share it, the wording softens to "3 of the 4 … worth checking that recorder first."

A list of four dead cameras invites four truck rolls. Naming the shared recorder turns the same data into one hypothesis, and the hedged variant matters as much as the confident one — an overconfident diagnosis that sends someone to the wrong box costs more than no diagnosis at all.

Drill-down. Clicking a center name — or the "Open in center view" action inside a camera's modal — does three things at once: sets the session's active center, lands on that center's existing Cameras page, and leaves an "← All centers" breadcrumb. No new UI is invented for the destination; it is the page that already exists. Clicking a square opens the camera instead, so the two gestures never collide.

Single-center users see none of this. With one assigned center there is nothing to aggregate, so the Camera health toggle and the zoom-out link are both absent rather than present-and-pointless. The prototype's "Single-center user" control demonstrates the rule.

Where I departed from the mockup

Five deliberate changes. Each is reversible if you disagree.

1 · Light, not dark

Your call, and the right one — it's the only way the page belongs to the same product as Cameras / Rooms / Classes. The dark mockup informed the content and hierarchy, nothing else. The video frame in the drawer is a light neutral placeholder rather than a black box.

2 · A drawer, not a modal

Clicking a block opens a non-blocking panel on the right instead of a centered modal. Checking cameras is repetitive — you rarely inspect one and stop. A modal makes you close it to see the grid again; the drawer keeps the grid visible and adds ← / → to walk the center's cameras in place. Below 640px it becomes a bottom sheet.

3 · Status has a shape, not only a color

Red and green are the classic failure case for the ~8% of men with deuteranopia, and this board's entire meaning is carried by red-versus-green. So each status also has a texture, and the row states its counts in words regardless.

4 · Rank numbers on the rows

When the sort is "most issues" or "longest outage", each row shows its rank. It makes the ordering an explicit claim the user can verify rather than an ordering they have to trust — and it makes the ranking visibly change after a refresh.

5 · Summary tiles above the list

Four numbers — centers with issues, cameras offline, degraded, longest outage — that double as one-click filters. Not a dashboard; the smallest possible answer to "how bad is it right now" before anyone scrolls. In single-center scope they turn read-only, because filtering one center by its own status does nothing.

The refresh contract

A page that rewrites itself every 30 seconds is hostile unless it follows rules. These are the rules I built:

  • The countdown is visible. A ring drains toward the next read, and the subtitle says how old the current data is — "read 12s ago". A live view that can't say when it last succeeded isn't a live view.
  • Pause is real, and labeled. Paused shows "showing a frozen view", not a stopped clock.
  • It stops refreshing on its own when the tab is hidden, when a camera drawer is open, and while someone is typing in the filter box. Every skipped poll is a query the read replica never serves.
  • Ranking never changes under the cursor. If a refresh would reorder rows while the pointer or the keyboard focus is inside the list, the new order is held and a pill offers "Ranking changed — reorder". It applies itself the moment the user leaves. Reordering under a moving cursor is how people click the wrong camera.
  • Page position survives a refresh — page number, scroll, filters, and the focused block in the camera grid.
  • Failure is stated, not hidden. A failed read leaves the last good data on screen behind an amber banner naming its age, and never shows an empty board.
  • Screen readers get a polite summary after each read — "3 of 6 centers have camera issues, 9 cameras offline" — not 250 individual block changes.

Keyboard and assistive access

  • 252 cameras would be 252 tab stops. Instead each center's grid is one tab stop with a roving focus: arrows move between cameras, Home / End jump to the ends, Enter opens the drawer, Esc closes it and returns focus to the block you came from.
  • Every block has an accessible name that includes the duration: "Camera 14, Nap room B, offline for 1h 05m".
  • The hover card also appears on focus, so keyboard users get the same quick facts.
  • Informational text sits at 4.5:1 or better; the muted #9ca3af from the style guide is used only on genuinely disabled controls.
  • Motion respects prefers-reduced-motion.

Sorting, filtering, pagination

ControlBehavior
Default sortOffline count desc → degraded count desc → longest outage desc → name A–Z. Healthy centers therefore fall below the troubled ones and sort alphabetically among themselves, which is exactly the brief's rule, expressed as one comparator.
Other sortsLongest outage, center name A–Z, most cameras. The active sort is shown in the control, never implied.
FiltersFree text over center name and store number; status; brand; state; parent-facing. Each applied filter appears as a removable chip with a "Clear all", and the result line always states what survived: "14 centers · 232 cameras · 19 needing attention".
Pagination10 / 25 / 50 per page, server-side, hidden entirely when there is only one page. Changing pages preserves filters and sort.
ScopeDefaults to the single center the user is viewing, per the brief. Multi-center users get a one-time nudge — "Your role can see 14 centers" — so the wider view is discoverable without being imposed.

All of it should live in the URL. A regional manager who finds three centers down wants to paste that exact board into Slack, and a bookmark of "my brand, offline only, by outage age" is the feature people will actually use every morning.

Notes for whoever builds it

  • Don't query camera_assessment_logs_v2 directly. "Latest row per camera" over a growing log, for every poll, by every admin, is the query that will hurt. Maintain a camera_current_status projection — one row per camera, updated on heartbeat, holding status, status_since, and assessed_at. Sorting centers by offline count and paginating them then becomes an indexed read instead of a windowed scan.
  • Compute the counts in SQL, per page of centers. Never ship 20,000 camera rows to the client to count them there.
  • Make the poll cheap. Return an ETag and let unchanged reads answer 304 — most 30-second polls change nothing.
  • Distinguish "offline" from "we haven't heard". If assessed_at is older than the 30-minute heartbeat, the camera's status is unknown, not normal, and it should not be counted as healthy. We scoped the legend to three values; this case still needs a decision (see question 1).
  • Scope every query by the user's access server-side. The scope control is a convenience, never the security boundary.
  • Durations are computed from status_since, not from the client clock, so a stale tab can't invent a 6-hour outage.

Open questions

1

Should the Camera health toggle carry a count?

The concept spec called a live count badge the main discoverability driver — with zero issues the feature has no reason to be clicked, so the number does the arguing. The visual reference for the toggle has no badge, and I built it without one. The middle option is to append the number only when it is non-zero, so the chip reads "Camera health · 19" on a bad day and stays clean on a good one. Worth deciding deliberately, because as built the feature is silent about needing attention until someone opens it.

2

What should the board do with a camera whose last heartbeat is older than 30 minutes?

Right now it keeps whatever status it last reported, which quietly counts a silent camera as healthy. My recommendation is a fourth neutral-gray state, "not reporting" — but that widens the legend you scoped to three.

3

Fleet version: does drilling into a center persist as the session's selected center?

If it persists, navigating to Requests afterwards stays in that center — which matches the existing mental model of "you are now in this center's context". If it is a one-off jump, the selection reverts when you leave. The prototype persists it and says so in a banner on arrival. I recommend persisting; the alternative means the app's active center can differ from the page you are looking at, which is harder to explain than it is to build.

4

Are "last checked" times shown in the viewer's timezone or the center's?

A Dallas admin looking at an Oklahoma center will read them differently. Relative times ("27m ago") dodge the question, which is why the prototype uses them; absolute timestamps in the drawer will need an answer.

5

Is camera order within a center stable and meaningful?

The grid is only trustworthy if a room's block stays in the same position between visits. If cameras have a display order, the grid should use it; if not, the block positions are effectively random and worth ordering by room.

6

Does "degraded" have reasons behind it?

If the log distinguishes low bitrate from dropped frames from a bad angle, the drawer should say which — it's the difference between "call the vendor" and "nudge the camera".

History, saved-clip playback, and an admin display mode are out of scope for this pass, but the sub-nav already reserves Live, History, Saved Clips, and Admin Display as decorative tabs — grouped and separated by dividers — so their eventual home is visible now rather than invented later. The same pattern App.jsx uses for Billing and Permissions.
